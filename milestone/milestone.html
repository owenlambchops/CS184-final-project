Accomplishments So Far

Water Droplets Simulation

We have set up Python and C++ environments to simulate water droplet dynamics on a mesh, currently utilizing a Python script and notebook for development and testing. Our primary focus is implementing the position and velocity models described by Zhang et al. (2012). This model accounts for surface tension, viscosity dampening, local and global volume correction, water-to-surface friction, and hydrophobicity, which is modeled by applying additional forces to correct boundary angles.

Implementation Challenges

Zhang et al. implemented a numerical simulation method based on a point-lumped mass surface representation with explicit Euler integration. While our implementation successfully simulates general fluid-like behavior, the droplet currently struggles to maintain a round shape and instead collapses into a flat puddle. Testing suggests this may be due to a low surface tension coefficient (gamma), high lumped mass density or gravity, or a large number of vertices (which contributes to increased total mass).

Attempting to fix the flattening by increasing the surface tension leads to numerically unstable or underdamped behavior; the restoring forces from the surface tension accumulate oscillations that eventually break the droplet mesh. We can dampen these oscillations by increasing the viscosity beyond the paper's recommended range (0.3-0.5), but this is not ideal as it prevents us from faithfully reproducing the original simulation results.

Component Verification

To isolate and verify our physical parameters, we ran the following tests:

  • Surface Tension: We simulated an isolated mesh in a zero-gravity environment. As expected, the vertices self-distributed evenly along the surface. To further test this, we initialized the droplet as a rectangular mesh and observed it correctly collapse into a spherical shape.
  • Surface Contact Angle: We tested the droplet in a gravity environment across different contact angle ranges. We observed a notable effect on the roundness of the droplet, consistent with the literature. Unfortunately, likely due to low mesh resolution or inadequate parameter tuning, we could not discern the clear droplet-to-surface boundary shown in the original paper.

Surface Tension

Surface backends are implemented in PlaneSurface (flat plane with analytically exact closest-point and raycast) and HeightFieldSurface (bilinearly interpolated height grid with finite-difference gradient normals and sphere-marching raycast), both satisfying the ISurface interface used throughout the solver and interaction pipeline. Droplet topology changes are implemented in MergeSplitController, which detects proximity-based merging and elongation-based splitting each frame, with DropletFactory handling volume-weighted blending for merge and principal-axis offset for split. In each substep, we recompute per-droplet derived quantities (vertex normals, center of mass, average velocity, footprint radius, elongation ratio, and principal axis).

GPU Rendering

DropletGpuCache syncs VAO/VBO/EBO buffers against the live droplet list each frame, and the scene renders with Phong shading via two GLSL programs. Mouse drag interaction projects through Picker raycasting against the active surface into a radial falloff DragForceField. ExperimentLogger records per-frame sim time, render time, and volume error to CSV. We are working on the UiController (Dear ImGui sliders for solver, render, material, and merge/split parameters) and the RefractiveRenderer three-pass pipeline (scene color/depth, droplet G-buffer, full-screen refraction composite) before the full feature set is complete.

Reflection on Progress

Our April 20 milestone target was a working simulation environment, correct water drop behavior, and a basic functioning interactive UI. We have met all of these goals on the physics and interaction side and completed the solver, force fields, surface backends, merge/split, and GPU mesh pipeline. Our next goal is to complete refractive shading composite pass and improve UI controller.

Next Steps

Our next milestone is to finish the rendering and polish work while keeping the solver stable. From Apr 20–25, we will complete refractive shading, including FBO creation, the G-buffer pass, and the full-screen refraction composite shader, while also wiring up Dear ImGui parameter sliders and beginning the project report.

From Apr 26–May 2, we plan to add real-time caustics, continue merge/split polish, expose customizable fluid parameters through the UI, and build a height-field surface demo. During this window we will also finalize the deliverable video and project website.

The current simulation stack remains a C++ / OpenGL 3.3 implementation driven by SimulationSystem and SingleDropletSolver. Each droplet is advanced per frame with explicit Euler integration and configurable substeps, applying cotangent-weighted surface tension, Laplacian viscosity, volume-stiffness correction, contact-line forces for advancing and receding angles, and external force sampling. Surface collisions use pushout, bounce, and sliding friction, and volume preservation is tracked with signed tetrahedral summation over the open mesh closed by a fan-triangulated boundary cap.

If solver instability remains a problem, we may revisit alternative integration methods, but our immediate focus is on completing the refractive rendering and UI work before the May 4 final deliverable deadline and the May 5 presentation.
