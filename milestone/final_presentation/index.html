Real-Time Lagrangian Water Droplet Simulation

cs184 Spring 2026

Kevin Ying, Haoyi Yu, Owen Lam, Yoko Furukawa

Abstract

This project implements a physically-inspired water droplet simulation framework. Our primary goal is to replicate the novel approach presented by Zhang et al. for animating small-scale water droplets. Instead of relying on computationally expensive 3D volumetric Eulerian grids, their method employs a real-time, Lagrangian deformable surface model. The authors' approach relied on a particle-based surface mesh that uses a lumped mass matrix. It models surface tension through mean curvature flow (approximated via a discrete Laplace-Beltrami operator), strictly enforces advancing and receding contact angles to simulate hysteresis, and approximates surface friction using a velocity threshold. For this project, we successfully reproduced their following methodology:

  • Replicate this Lagrangian surface model.
  • Achieve stable water droplet dynamics.
  • Implement robust mesh merging and splitting.
  • Ensure the entire framework runs in real time.

We then extend upon their work by implementing:

  • Real-time caustics.
  • Higher performance using velocity Verlet integration.
  • Interactive user interface.

To support these goals, the framework features interactive manipulation and GPU-accelerated rendering, organized into five modular subsystems:

  • Simulation System: Droplet physics and numerical integration.
  • Surface Representation: Planar and heightfield surfaces.
  • Rendering Pipeline: GPU mesh management and refractive rendering.
  • Interaction System: Mouse picking and drag interaction.
  • UI Layer: Parameter editing and scene control.

Technical approach

Simulation system Kevin Serious Ying Owen Antonio Lam

1. Droplet Representation

a. droplet_template

The droplet template stores the rest-state mesh topology. The droplet mesh is generated as a spherical cap using radial rings and angular segments, producing a smooth dome-like shape that approximates a water droplet. Each template stores rest vertices, triangle faces, and boundary loop indices, which are important for contact-line physics, volume preservation, and droplet splitting.

Each droplet maintains dynamic per-vertex positions and velocities, while updateDerived() computes geometric properties such as vertex normals, center of mass, and principal deformation axes.

b. droplet_factory

The droplet factory generates droplets by first sampling the target surface and then transforming the template's local coordinates into the surface's tangent space. This allows newly spawned droplets to align naturally with arbitrary surface orientations and geometry.

2. Merging / splitting

When droplets merge when centers are sufficiently close (distance < factor * combinedRadius). The system conserves total volume and approximates momentum conservation by blending the droplets' centers and velocities using volume-weighted averages.

Splitting occurs when 1) volume exceeds threshold and 2) elongation becomes large. Two smaller droplets are spawned in opposite directions to mimic capillary breakup behavior.

3. Physics operators

The simulation is built from modular physics operators that update vertex position, acceleration and velocities for different fluid behaviors:

  • external forces
  • Collisions
  • Viscosity
  • surface tension
  • contact dynamics
  • volume preservation

External forces factor in acceleration due to gravity and user interaction update vertex velocities, the collision operator resolves surface penetration, friction, and adhesion to produce realistic sliding motion.

ViscosityOperator adds linear damping to the vertices from eta viscosity and mu damping scaled by laplacian. An additional non-linear velocity damping CurvatureFlowOperator applies mean curvature flow on each vertex calculated using cotangent weights to estimate Laplacian smoothing to simulate internal fluid diffusion and surface tension, while additional restoring forces help preserve droplet volume over time.

ContactLineOperator: To work around relying on physical mechanisms to model water droplet hysteresis, Zhang et al. added an additional non-physical force on boundary vertices to force advancing and receding contact angles.

Lastly, VolumeCorrector operator corrects volume by adding an additional velocity to each vertex based on the current volume vs initial volume difference, rescaling vertices to maintain stable droplet shape and volume. Due to the small size of water droplets, only global volume correction was applied, since while local volume correction base, which apply local vertex adjustments based on lumped area vs total area ratio.

4. SingleDropletSolver

The SingleDropletSolver advances each droplet in following order:

  1. recompute derived geometry
  2. Viscosity
  3. curvature flow
  4. contact angle forces
  5. external forces
  6. Integration
  7. collision projection
  8. volume correction

We used semi-implicit Euler integration combined with operator splitting, allowing different physical effects to be applied on each substep. During each update, the solver recomputes geometric properties before applying viscosity, curvature flow, contact forces, external forces, integration, collision handling, and volume correction in a fixed order to improve numerical stability. The semi-implicit integration step updates vertex positions using X += dt * U, providing a simple and efficient approach suitable for real-time simulation.

1. Plane Surface

The plane surface provides analytical projection, analytical ray intersection, and exact normals.

Ray-plane intersection: t = (origin - ray.origin).dot(normal) / denom

2. Heightfield Surface

Bilinear Sampling — Heights are interpolated from grid samples.

Gradient-Based Normals — Normals are calculated using slopes approximated using finite differences: (-dhdx, 1, -dhdz)

Sphere Tracing Raycast — The heightfield uses iterative signed-distance stepping: t += abs(distance) * 0.5

3. Mesh Representation

  • Droplet mesh (droplet_template.h): Closed mesh generated as a cube subdivided using loop subdivision
  • Mesh edges stored in a map, faces and vertices stored as n×3 MatXi matrix
  • Surface area calculated by summing face areas: ½(a−b)×(c−b)
  • Lumped area per vertex: ⅓ × Σ(neighbor face areas)

4. Mesh Operations

We faced challenges with mesh operations while implementing mesh merge behavior. Since we built the simulation from scratch and avoided external libraries like CGAL, we used a Ray-Cast Shrinkwrap algorithm to approximate mesh Boolean union.

This method first projected a spherical template onto the intersecting hulls of two droplets. Then, rays cast from the center of mass through each vertex were tested for intersections with the droplet surfaces using the Möller–Trumbore algorithm. Vertices were adjusted to the farthest intersection or, for concave regions, snapped to the nearest known vertex. We then applied the volume preservation on the combined volume (Vnew = V1 + V2). This approach bypassed the need for Boolean union libraries, maintained a closed mesh topology with smooth fuse boundary, while integrating nicely into our existing simulation.

Rendering Pipeline Haoyi Yu

1. GPU Mesh Buffers

  • GpuMeshBuffer manages VAOs, VBOs, and EBOs
  • Positions and normals of vertices are updated dynamically every frame: glBufferData(... GL_DYNAMIC_DRAW)
  • Rendering uses triangles: glDrawElements(GL_TRIANGLES, ...)

2. GPU Cache

The cache maintains GPU buffers per droplet ID. New droplets allocate GPU resources; removed droplets free buffers automatically.

3. Refractive Renderer

Renderer is structured into the following passes:

  • Light-Space Droplet Buffer Pass — Renders droplets from the HDRI sun direction and stores light-view normals, front depth, and back depth. (renderLightDropletBuffers())
  • Caustic Map Pass — Uses the light-space droplet buffers to project refracted photon splats onto the finite plane. (renderCausticMap())
  • Scene Pass — Renders the HDRI background and support plane, including projected caustics and droplet shadow. (renderSceneColorDepth())
  • Droplet G-Buffer Pass — Renders camera-view droplet front normals and front depth. (renderDropletGBuffer())
  • Droplet Back-Depth Pass — Renders camera-view droplet back depth for thickness estimation. (renderDropletBackDepth())
  • Composite Pass — Combines scene color, screen-space refraction, Fresnel environment reflection, and thickness absorption. (compositeDroplets())
  • Wireframe Debug Pass — Optionally overlays droplet mesh wireframe in wireframe debug mode. (compositeDroplets())

Drag interaction

The drag interaction system allows users to manipulate droplets directly using mouse input. It's achieved by the DragInteractor, which connects user input to the force-field system via void DragInteractor::update(...).

The update loop performs three major tasks: detecting mouse release events and disabling dragging, generating a picking ray from the camera, and raycasting against the simulation surface. If a valid intersection occurs:

  • dragField_->setActive(true);
  • dragField_->setCenter(hit.position);

The drag force field becomes active and follows the cursor-projected world position.

UI Interface Haoyi Yu

The UI exposes simulation and rendering parameters in real time. Built using Dear ImGui.

  • Debugging shading effects: Final view, thickness view (to debug boundary angles), vertex normal map, isolated caustics effect map, isolated environment map, wireframe view
  • Droplet Creation: Users can set spawn coordinates and create droplets interactively
  • Gravity editing: Users can edit gravity vectors live
  • Plane tilt controls: The surface normal of the plane is generated from pitch and roll. Users can tilt plane from −80° to 80° to simulate the movement of raindrops on the slanted surface.
  • Material controls: Users can edit parameters including surface tension, friction, viscous damping, contact angles, volume stiffness to explore different fluid behaviours.
  • Solver Controls: Users can toggle collision, viscosity, curvature flow, contact angle simulation, and volume correction.

Challenges

We initially encountered a critical numerical stability issue in the simulation. We observed that droplets struggled to maintain a stable spherical cap shape, often collapsing or jittering under gravity. This behaviour was likely caused by an incorrect volume correction implementation. Zhang et al. proposed two volume correction operator methods: local Correction, which preserves fine details by adjusting velocity via u'i = ui − ᾱini, and Global Correction, which ensures exact volume conservation by uniformly offsetting the mesh by d = ΔV/A.

For the observation, we used γ = 2000.0, μ = 16.0, η = 8.0, ρ = 1.0, radius = 0.3.

Initially, to apply smooth volume correction, we added a "volume stiffness" factor within the curvature flow operator, which disrupted the droplet's essential geometry, and unstable volume loss was caused by initial sensitive local volume correction implementations. We fixed it by implementing a dedicated VolumeCorrector operator to isolate volume conservation logic and properly scaling the Laplacian vertex mass, and calling it last.

We also found that hyper-parameter tuning was important. With gravity set as −9.81 (meters per second), capillary actions only come into effect for small droplet sizes (diameter ≤ 3mm). This partially explains our observation for radius 3cm, where the edge lengths may have caused the torque generated by gravity to overcome the strength of the mean curvature flow.

With a much smaller radius, we found that the droplet showed a more visible boundary angle, but became more underdamped. By reducing gravity, we were able to allow the surface tension to pull the droplet into its final shape.

To further improve stability, we also added clamps to ensure velocities and translations are well-bounded, and added an argument to disable local volume correction. We found that while local volume correction can preserve more capillary effects and details (Zhang et al., 2012), the higher frequency surface features sometimes degraded droplet stability. This separation of concerns prevented the artificial stiffness during the curvature flow step.

While with those changes, the droplet is stable, due to numerical imprecisions and the lack of physical energy loss modeling associated with point-based surface modelling techniques, the droplet does not stabilize at an equilibrium. Velocity-based damping terms were the most effective and practical solution. See also: Kinetic energy recovery and interface hysteresis.
